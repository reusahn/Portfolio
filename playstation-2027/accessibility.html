Selected work / Built projects

Interaction is where accessibility begins.

Three completed production contexts provide different kinds of evidence: player-state logic, multimodal response, and emotionally sensitive real-time embodiment. The accessibility opportunities are presented as next design studies, not past accessibility-study results.

01 / Built: 2024 · Unity VR · Interactive NPC

Escape Metro

A VR escape game in which the player must retrieve a protected password from an AI character. Conversation becomes the core puzzle, linked to progress in a deterministic game world.

What I built: a conversational NPC interaction within a Unity VR escape structure, joining model responses to game progression and embodied exploration.

Accessibility research angle: conversational puzzles can impose speech, cognitive, memory, language, and motor burdens. I would examine text-first input, repeatable goals, transcript persistence, error recovery, and how to communicate uncertain NPC responses without disclosing the answer.

02 / Built: 2024 · Unity · physical computing

AI ZOO

An embodied installation that translates typed language and hand gestures into generated voice, physical motor movement, lighting, and perceived response.

What I built: an interaction pipeline joining Unity, Python, AI outputs, a Leap Motion interface, and Arduino-controlled physical feedback.

Accessibility research angle: multiple output channels are not inherently accessible. A genuine review must test whether any single channel is required, whether critical information persists, and whether a player can choose an alternative input route.

03 / Built: 2024–26 · Real-time characters · VR

Shininho

Reconstructing a particular person for a virtual encounter required more than a functioning rig. A first prototype moved, but lacked a recognizable identity. That failure informed a revised character pipeline.

What I built: scan cleanup, geometry preparation, retargeting, a real-time character pipeline, and VR presentation.

Accessibility research angle: animation speed, camera movement, spatial cues, and performance readability affect how players interpret character presence. Proposed work would test comfort and alternatives rather than assert accessibility outcomes from visual realism.

Original interactive sample / Implemented on this website

Signal Path: one goal, multiple ways to act.

A small turn-based navigation interaction created to make input redundancy and perceptual choices inspectable. It is a design prototype, not a validated accessible game or formal participant study.

Play it, not just read about it.

Move a token through a short five-position path and avoid a blocked position. The interface includes keyboard and on-screen controls, text state descriptions, a live-status region, high contrast, larger type, and reduced-motion options.

Success and failure are conveyed by text as well as color. The same core mechanic can be completed with different input methods.

Open the working prototype ↗

What this proves—and what it does not

Demonstrates: implemented browser controls, explicit focus handling, status messaging, and preference toggles on a small game interaction.

Does not demonstrate yet: PAS compliance, accessibility certification, reduced barriers for actual players with disabilities, or validated playtest outcomes.

Next: recruit opt-in participants, conduct an expert review, measure completion and recovery, record points of friction, and revise the prototype.

Role-aligned workflow

How I would support an accessibility team.

Grounded in the responsibilities of an accessibility intern: identify issues, document them, collaborate on design alternatives, and turn qualitative observations into clear engineering work.

Observe the play experience

Break down tasks into input, comprehension, execution, feedback, and recovery. Identify which step creates the obstacle without assuming one disability experience stands for another.

Describe the barrier

Record reproduction steps, trigger conditions, affected player goals, configurable settings, and alternative pathways. Separate observed effects from the team's initial hypothesis.

Test a design change

Collaborate with UX and engineering on a testable adjustment. Check completion, errors, recovery time, participant preference, and whether the fix changes the intended play experience.

PROJECT STUDY A

Accessibility review: Escape Metro's dialogue gate

Scenario: player must persuade an NPC to provide a password to leave. Possible barriers include spoken input, unpredictable system replies, inaccessible transcripts, ambiguous progress, and VR movement demands.

Proposed alternatives: selectable text / voice paths where feasible, persistent transcript, one-action replay, adjustable interaction time, optional control remapping, and a non-color-dependent progress cue. The prototype must preserve the challenge rather than simply reveal the password.

Proposed measures: success rate by input pathway; number of repeated attempts; time to recover from a misunderstood reply; player-reported clarity; and annotated issue severity. These measurements have not been collected.

PROJECT STUDY B

Multimodal fallback: AI ZOO

Scenario: physical gesture and sound convey interactive state. A player may be unable to use or perceive the dominant channel.

Proposed alternatives: text equivalents for speech, physical button alternatives for gestures, persistent state labels, adjustable feedback intensity, and clear system-response timing. Audit whether switching an input channel changes the intended expressive outcome.

Proposed measures: discoverability of the alternative, action success, missed state changes, repeat interactions, and participant-reported sense of control.
