CINEMATIC DECISIONS
01 / ENSEMBLE FIRST

Start by making the relationship between the three characters readable.

Because the story depends on three strangers sharing a confined space, the first responsibility of coverage is spatial clarity: who is near whom, who is separated, where attention is moving, and when the group should be read as a single image rather than as three independent portraits.

02 / CHARACTER FOCUS

Move from group information toward individual psychological information.

As the sequence becomes more character-specific, the visual problem changes. The frame no longer needs to explain the whole raft; it needs to isolate the person whose reaction, silence, or internal state carries the moment. This is the same coverage logic I would apply when moving from gameplay context into a character close-up.

CONTEXTEstablish the ensemble and spatial relationship.
FOCUSShift attention toward the active character.
REACTIONHold enough time for the emotional information to register.
RETURNReconnect the character moment to the larger group.
03 / SHOT DURATION

A shot should stay only as long as its information needs.

My directing approach is sequence-based rather than beauty-frame based. Camera position, blocking, and shot duration are judged in relation to the shots before and after them. The edit should not cut before an important reaction is readable, but it should also not remain after the shot has stopped adding information.

04 / TRANSFER TO GAME CAPTURE

The scene type changes, but the decision framework stays the same.

For a game capture assignment, the subject might be a combat beat, a dialogue scene, a character reveal, a traversal moment, or a marketing beauty shot. I would begin with the same questions: what must the viewer understand first, what scale of shot communicates that information, where should the eye land, what movement is justified, and what coverage is needed to cut the sequence cleanly.
