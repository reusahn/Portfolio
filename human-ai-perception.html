VIRTUAL HUMANS · PERCEPTION · PRESENCE · HUMAN–AI INTERACTION

Between Human Perception and Machine Interpretation

I use virtual humans as experimental media for studying how people attribute presence, identity, emotion, agency, and reality to computational bodies. The work begins in digital-human production but asks a different question: after a face, body, voice, and motion become technically convincing, what still determines whether a person experiences the figure as socially real?

Research stance. This page presents a practice-based framework, observations from my own projects, and proposed experimental directions. It does not present those observations as completed controlled human-subjects evidence. My goal is to translate production encounters into measurable HCI questions.
01 / RESEARCH QUESTIONS

Technical fidelity is only one variable in virtual human experience.

My current questions focus on the perceptual and relational criteria people use when they decide that a virtual person feels believable, familiar, intentional, or alive.

Q1

Presence beyond visual fidelity

Which behavioral and temporal cues contribute to social presence once appearance is already convincing?

Q2

Relational recognition

How does prior knowledge of a person change judgments of likeness, authenticity, and embodied identity?

Q3

Agency and vulnerability

Do fatigue, risk, irreversibility, and physical consequence change how viewers attribute aliveness or empathy to an artificial agent?

Q4

Machine-mediated reality

When generated representations are coherent and persuasive, how do people negotiate conflicts between visual evidence, memory, and testimony?

02 / WORKING FRAMEWORK

The virtual human as a perceptual probe.

My practice treats representational failure as information. When a digital human is technically accurate yet perceptually incomplete, the mismatch can expose latent criteria in human judgment.

MODEL A / PRODUCTION → PERCEPTION
Human → Virtual Human → Perceptual / Relational Layers → Image + Sound → Machine Representation

The virtual human sits close enough to the human form that its failures become diagnostic: the missing element may be historical context, remembered behavior, relational testimony, bodily rhythm, or irreversible time rather than polygon count or texture resolution.

MODEL B / MACHINE → HUMAN JUDGMENT
Machine Interpretation → Generated Representation → Human Judgment

The inverse question asks what happens when machine-generated images, voices, or identities arrive before human verification. Here the research focus shifts from realism itself to perceptual authority: what evidence people trust when representations and lived knowledge disagree.

Projects become research instruments when production questions meet human judgment.

These cases are not interchangeable demonstrations of “AI.” Each isolates a different tension between computational representation and the way people recognize human presence.

Can simulated distress become socially consequential?

AI ZOO synchronizes a digital human, synthetic voice, dialogue, physical motion, lighting, and audience proximity. The installation provides a practice-based setting for asking how people attribute emotion, intention, and vulnerability to a computational body.

SHININHO · RELATIONAL RECOGNITION

Who decides whether a reconstruction is “the person”?

Scan-derived geometry and performance capture can approximate appearance and motion, but familiar observers possess additional evidence: remembered gestures, timing, affect, and bodily habits. This creates a gap between measurable fidelity and relational recognition.

GANG SE-HWANG · HISTORICAL EMBODIMENT

What cannot be reconstructed from records alone?

A historical portrait can support facial reconstruction, costume research, and animation, yet archival accuracy does not automatically reproduce the embodied habits of another period. The case separates visual reconstruction from cultural and temporal presence.

04 / TOWARD EMPIRICAL STUDY

Turning practice-based observations into measurable HCI questions.

The next step is not to make larger philosophical claims, but to construct controlled manipulations that separate appearance, motion, identity, context, and agent behavior.

PROPOSED MANIPULATION 01

Appearance fidelity × motion timing

Hold identity and geometry stable while varying micro-timing, gaze, breathing, and gesture latency; measure social presence, perceived naturalness, and agency.

PROPOSED MANIPULATION 02

Familiar vs unfamiliar observers

Compare likeness and authenticity judgments from people with different relational knowledge of the represented person.

PROPOSED MANIPULATION 03

Reversible vs vulnerable agent states

Manipulate fatigue, degradation, recovery, and consequence while holding visual appearance constant; study perceived aliveness, empathy, and moral concern.

PROPOSED MANIPULATION 04

Representation vs testimony conflict

Vary whether generated visual evidence agrees or conflicts with contextual information; measure trust, memory, confidence, and source attribution.

05 / REPRESENTATION → HUMAN STUDY

4D human research can become experimental instrumentation.

My technical and human-centered research are not separate tracks. Controllable human representations can provide the precision needed to study perception without rebuilding an avatar for every experimental condition.

Why controllable 4D humans matter for HCI

If deformation, motion, appearance, and semantic body regions can be manipulated independently, a virtual human can become a controlled stimulus rather than only a finished character. That makes representation quality useful not just for visual fidelity, but for experimental isolation.

  • Manipulate one body region while holding identity constant.
  • Change motion timing without changing appearance.
  • Separate learned deformation artifacts from perceptual response.
  • Compare human judgments across semantically controlled avatar states.

Current technical direction

My 4D human work examines learned skinning, SMPL/LBS semantics, Gaussian human representations, semantic locality, and controllability. The longer-term aim is to connect these representation tools with studies of embodiment, social presence, identity, and human–AI interaction.
