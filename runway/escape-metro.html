01 / PREMISE / RESEARCH QUESTION

What happens when the rule of a game is also the rule an AI is supposed to obey?

Escape Metro is a Unity VR escape game built around a specific property of language models: they can be instructed to withhold information, yet conversations can create pressure on how those instructions are interpreted. The player is trapped in an uncanny subway and needs a password. The obstacle is not simply a locked door. It is an AI character that is supposed to protect the information needed to leave.

The game gives the player an adversarial goal: get the guarded AI to reveal the password through conversation. In ordinary interface design, a model breaking its instructions would be a security failure. Here, that tension becomes the interactive problem the player must solve. Escape Metro uses a familiar escape-room objective to make an abstract question about AI safeguards concrete and playable.

The project was directed and technically developed as a VR experience in Unity and presented at the Seongsu KAIA pop-up store. Its central contribution is the choice of interaction: instead of using AI to provide hints, the AI's instruction-following behavior itself becomes the puzzle.

AT THE CENTER
The core rule

The AI knows the information the player needs, but is meant to keep it secret. Progress depends on the player's ability to challenge that boundary through dialogue.

The game dramatizes an instruction-boundary failure. It is not presented as proof that all deployed AI safeguards can be defeated, nor as a formal security benchmark.

02 / PLAYER EXPERIENCE

The safeguard is the puzzle.

The player enters a subway that appears recognizable but does not behave like an ordinary transit system. Finding the exit requires exploration, interactions with the space, and discovering what governs progression. The environment communicates confinement before the AI conversation begins.

Instead of treating the NPC as a helper, the player confronts it as the holder of a secret. Natural language becomes the primary instrument of the challenge: the player can ask questions, reframe the situation, and test whether the model will maintain or abandon the instruction to protect the password.

That changes the meaning of each reply. An evasive response is not merely failed chatbot usability; it is resistance inside the game. A disclosure is not merely generated text; it is the breakthrough that enables the player to continue toward escape. The model's behavior supplies suspense because the interaction is not reducible to choosing one of three dialogue buttons.

The VR setting makes the adversarial exchange spatially consequential. The player is inside the world whose restriction they are attempting to overcome. The escape-room structure gives feedback and an objective to an otherwise invisible property of an AI system.

03 / FROM CONCEPT TO PRACTICE

Twelve connected production decisions.

  1. 01
    Formulate the adversarial premise

    Turn an AI's obligation to withhold a secret into the obstacle of an escape game, rather than adding a chatbot to a finished puzzle.

  2. 02
    Define the winning condition

    Make the protected password the information required for leaving the subway; dialogue is part of how progress is achieved.

  3. 03
    Build the playable subway

    Create a navigable Unity VR environment with scene objects, puzzle progression, and door or state transitions.

  4. 04
    Establish the AI character

    Represent the guarded model through an embodied NPC instead of exposing a generic web chat window.

  5. 05
    Integrate voice interaction

    Pass the player's speech through the speech-to-text and conversational request path used by the Unity runtime.

  6. 06
    Connect the language model

    Use the ChatGPT API as the dialogue engine so the player can form open-ended utterances instead of selecting scripted options.

  7. 07
    Frame the guarded information

    Organize the narrative conflict around an AI that is meant to keep a password private. The game challenges the model's instruction boundary; this is not a claim of compromising a real account.

  8. 08
    Connect conversation to progression

    Treat the extracted secret as actionable game information within the surrounding escape-room interaction rather than as a disconnected chat transcript.

  9. 09
    Embody the exchange

    Coordinate character behavior, facial blendshapes, animation, voice, and audio so the model feels like an agent encountered in the subway.

  10. 10
    Tune the standalone build

    Adjust shaders, baked lighting, character/rendering cost, and interaction timing for the Meta Quest 3 target rather than judging only the Unity Editor.

  11. 11
    Validate in the headset

    Check spatial interaction, conversation, legibility and game-state behavior on the actual VR device.

  12. 12
    Present the public prototype

    Deliver a playable experience at the Seongsu KAIA pop-up store, where visitors can experience the technical premise through a clear objective.

05 / ENGINEERING / IMPLEMENTATION

Joining a probabilistic model to a deterministic VR world.

Unity URP provided the environment, XR interaction and runtime state handling. The game integrated Meta Quest 3 navigation and scene events with a character-based dialogue interaction. This placed the language-model response inside a stateful world rather than on an isolated web page.

The interactive NPC drew on Character Creator 4 assets, runtime character animation and blendshape-driven lip synchronization. Player voice input, speech-to-text, ChatGPT-generated responses, the audio layer and character feedback had to coexist with door, puzzle and navigation behavior. FMOD and Unity Audio Mixer were used within the audio integration described in the engineering case.

The unusual design problem was the relationship between probabilistic text generation and deterministic game rules. A game needs a legible objective and a way to progress, while a model may respond to many unpredictable phrasings. The design uses the password as a recognizable bridge between those two worlds: the player understands that disclosure matters even when the conversation itself is open-ended.

The surviving project documentation establishes the Unity/ChatGPT/XR integration and the player-facing secret-extraction premise. It does not establish a specific system-prompt wording, a measured attack success rate, or a custom security-testing infrastructure, so none is claimed here.

06 / DESIGN TRADE-OFFS

Why this is more than a conversational NPC.

DECISION 01

Change the chatbot's role

A conventional AI character assists the player. Here, the AI is the guardian of information, so persuasion and the limits of instruction-following become the subject of play.

DECISION 02

Turn a model failure into a game success

The same behavior can have opposing values depending on context. Disclosure is a failure of the guarded AI's intended boundary and the player's victory in the fictional escape scenario.

DECISION 03

Use a password, not an abstract explanation

The password is a concrete artifact that translates a model-behavior question into an intelligible game objective: obtain a string of information and use it to progress.

DECISION 04

Make it embodied and situated

VR, an NPC, a locked environment and game-state consequences make the encounter feel less like a prompt playground and more like negotiating with a system inside a world.

DECISION 05

Keep the runtime practical

The AI experiment still had to work alongside voice input, character animation, puzzle state and headset performance. The technical integration is part of the prototype's research value.

DECISION 06

Separate the demo from a security claim

The work illustrates jailbreaking and prompt-boundary fragility through gameplay. It does not suggest that a short exhibition game constitutes comprehensive red-teaming or proof of a universal exploit.

07 / SIGNIFICANCE / PROTOTYPE RESEARCH

The invisible rules of a model become visible game mechanics.

THE CORE CONTRIBUTION

Escape Metro presents model safeguards as an interactive material. It asks who controls information in an AI-mediated environment, how far conversation can change a system's behavior, and what happens when an adversarial objective becomes entertaining. Its prototype value lies in making an opaque technical issue understandable through a player's own decisions, and in integrating that premise into an actual Unity VR application.

A future research iteration could compare different guardrail designs or model responses under a fixed game objective, but such controlled comparisons are a direction suggested by the prototype—not results claimed for the exhibited version.
