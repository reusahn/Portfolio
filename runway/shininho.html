Shininho

01 / RESEARCH PROBLEM

If we reconstruct someone who has died, what would the person who loved them actually recognize—and feel?

Could an AI reconstruction preserve a relationship—or would it reveal what technology cannot recover about a person who is gone?

Shininho began with a question about the limits of digital remembrance. The subject is media artist Yaloo's late grandmother. If the traces she left behind—photographs, video, recordings, gestures, memories and a scan—could be assembled into an AI-driven conversational figure or new generated moving images, how might Yaloo feel when encountering that representation? Would recognition bring closeness, estrangement, comfort or something else? The question is not answered by technical realism alone.

In collaboration with Yaloo, I concentrated on a technically realizable first step: reconstructing her grandmother's scanned body as a controllable character capable of animation and real-time presentation. I extracted facial geometry from a combined volumetric scan, rebuilt symmetry for rigging, reduced and repaired the mesh, and tested textures, real-time hair and markerless animation in Unity. The figure moved. The premise could be prototyped.

But the first result exposed a contradiction at the center of the work. The character had become technically usable while losing the distinctive appearance that made it recognizably her grandmother. If a reconstruction is meant to carry someone's memory, a moving but generic body cannot be treated as success. I returned to the higher-resolution source and revised the workflow through Wrap and MetaHuman-compatible geometry, with renewed attention to the eyes, facial surfaces, gesture and performance.

THE KEY PROTOTYPE FAILUREEverything moved. But it no longer looked like the person.

The failure shifted the project from a generic real-time reconstruction to identity-preserving character production and an ongoing question about recognition.

02 / AUDIENCE / EXPERIENCE

A granddaughter's memory is a different measure of accuracy.

An audience may perceive a convincing digital human, but someone who knew the subject has a different reference: particular gestures, subtle timing, expressions and histories unavailable to a generic 3D model. Reconstructing Yaloo's grandmother made this difference between visual realism and relational recognition especially important. Within Yaloo's wider exhibition and performance work, my contribution is the technical character and animation pipeline rather than authorship of the whole artwork.

The later Jooan extension changes the frame from a directed screen to VR. Once viewers control where they look, character animation, lighting and visual effects must guide attention through space instead of relying on one fixed camera. Dusk lighting and Niagara hand-driven trails became compositional tools for an immersive performance.

03 / DEVELOPMENT

Twelve stages from rapid prototype to identity-preserving performance.

  1. 01
    Inspect the capture source

    Receive a single full-body volumetric mesh from an external studio and examine how to separate identity-bearing face data from body rigging needs.

  2. 02
    Rebuild symmetry in Maya

    Prepare an easier auto-rig test by extracting facial geometry, reconstructing bilateral shape, reducing and repairing the mesh.

  3. 03
    Construct a Unity feasibility build

    Integrate an AccuRIG character, scan-derived material, hair and test animation to determine whether it can move in real time.

  4. 04
    Recognize identity loss

    The prototype functions but reads as a generic character. This reveals that feasibility alone is the wrong final criterion.

  5. 05
    Return to original geometry

    Recover more specific likeness information from the higher-resolution scan rather than merely polishing the simplified prototype.

  6. 06
    Transfer into MetaHuman topology

    Use Wrap and a compatible animation structure while protecting scan-derived identity in facial geometry, UV and texture.

  7. 07
    Address facial details

    Revise eyes, skin appearance and other source-specific features whose loss changes recognition.

  8. 08
    Capture body performance

    Use OptiTrack / Motive sessions and supplemental MOVIN TRACIN data when wrist and finger tracking need additional coverage.

  9. 09
    Record the face separately

    Use iPhone facial capture with a Rokoko-mounted setup, then align its timing with body performance.

  10. 10
    Clean the solved motion

    Correct tracking spikes, foot float, joint artifacts and missing sections through MotionBuilder and Maya rather than accepting raw mocap.

  11. 11
    Retarget and assemble

    Bring corrected body and facial motion into the MetaHuman / Unreal pipeline for sequenced performance.

  12. 12
    Adapt for VR

    Recompose the experience around user-controlled gaze, spatial lighting and Niagara effects for the later Jooan presentation.

05 / TECHNICAL PRACTICE

Geometry, capture, animation and immersive direction.

The original question was emotional, but an experiment in remembrance needed an actual representation before any response could be explored. The Unity test established that scan data could become a rigged, textured and animated human. Its loss of likeness was revealing: preserving identity became part of the technical problem, rather than an aesthetic detail to fix at the end.

The replacement workflow used Wrap correspondence and MetaHuman-compatible production topology. It required more detailed attention to geometry, UV transfer, eye rendering and material response. The goal was to combine personal likeness with a reusable rig and animation ecosystem.

Performance capture adds independent sources of uncertainty. I handled OptiTrack body material, supplemental hand/finger capture, and separately recorded iPhone facial motion. A shared timing reference supported synchronization. MotionBuilder and Maya were used for cleanup and HumanIK retargeting, followed by Unreal assembly.

The VR extension required different directorial choices. Camera composition alone could no longer dictate where the audience looked; hand-attached Niagara ribbon effects and environmental light contributed to direction inside a space that viewers could examine freely.

06 / DESIGN DECISIONS

The choices behind the second prototype.

DECISION 01

When the person is remembered, a functioning rig is not enough

The first build animated successfully yet compromised personal likeness. That mismatch showed why someone who knew the deceased subject might judge the result very differently from an unfamiliar viewer.

DECISION 02

Preserve the person, not just the mesh

The revised geometry and material workflow retained details that generic reconstruction had flattened or changed.

DECISION 03

Use capture tools where they are strongest

Combining body, hand and facial streams allows more control than assuming one device will capture every useful detail.

DECISION 04

Choose targeted motion fixes

Foot drift, spikes and missing tracking require different responses. Cleanup and retargeting involve judgment about the final performance.

DECISION 05

Direct for a spatial audience

The VR version required an approach to timing, gaze and effects distinct from cinematic playback.

07 / WHY IT MATTERS

Recognition is a research constraint, not a cosmetic preference.

PROTOTYPE RESEARCH VALUE

The first prototype answered whether it could move. The second asked whether it could still be her.

Shininho moves from the technical problem of reconstruction to a human question about loss, memory and recognition. The existing work demonstrates a scanned, rigged, performed digital human and a revised real-time production pipeline. A possible next experiment—training a conversational system on available family material or building new generated video from an archive—raises the question of how Yaloo might respond. Such an AI memory model or measured emotional reaction is not claimed as completed work.
