OVERVIEW

From a technically successful prototype to an identity-preserving production digital human.

The project began with a full-body volumetric scan delivered as one combined face-and-body mesh. My first goal was feasibility rather than final fidelity: could the captured subject be converted into a riggable, animated, real-time digital human with the available source data? I extracted the face in Maya, rebuilt symmetry for auto-rigging, reduced and repaired the mesh, then built a working Unity prototype with rigging, transferred textures, a real-time hair setup, and video-derived markerless animation. Technically, the prototype worked. Creatively, it failed: it no longer felt like Shininho and instead read as a generic game character derived from her scan. That result changed the pipeline. I returned to the original high-resolution source, rebuilt the production character through Wrap and MetaHuman-compatible topology, preserved scan-derived texture information, and moved the final performance workflow into the MetaHuman ecosystem for Live Link, MetaHuman Animator, and Unreal Sequencer integration.

Task 01A / Source Breakdown

Separating a single full-body scan into a usable downstream character source

The original volumetric capture arrived as one continuous face-and-body mesh. Before I could test rigging or character deformation, I selected the facial polygons in Maya and extracted the face from the original geometry so the head and body could be handled more deliberately. The texture information already existed in the scan and was organized through the UV/texture layout, but the geometry itself was not delivered as a clean production character.

One combined full-body mesh containing both head and body.

Existing scan-derived texture information organized through the supplied UV/texture structure.

Selected the facial region in Maya and extracted it from the combined source mesh.

Head and body needed different downstream cleanup and rigging considerations.

The volumetric capture itself was performed by an external scan studio. My responsibility began with defining downstream character requirements and transforming the delivered scan into a usable real-time character pipeline.
Task 01B / Symmetry & Auto-Rig Preparation

Rebuilding symmetry because both body and facial auto-rigging depended on it

The source body was hunched and the two sides did not match closely enough for a fast automatic rigging workflow. I deleted one side, rebuilt bilateral symmetry with Maya Duplicate Special, reduced and retopologized the geometry, repaired local problem areas, and reassembled the character into a cleaner feasibility mesh.

Most of the auto-rigging workflow expected a substantially more symmetrical character than the original scan.

I was also preparing an ARKit-oriented facial auto-rig path, so left/right facial consistency mattered for the prototype.

Half-mesh rebuild → Duplicate Special symmetry → reduction / retopology → local repair → reassembly and smoothing.

A cleaner, more symmetrical mesh that could be sent through AccuRIG and tested rapidly in engine.

Task 01C / Unity Feasibility Prototype

Testing the complete real-time loop—not only whether the mesh could be rigged

The first character was intentionally a feasibility build. After auto-rigging, I brought the prototype into Unity and tested a much broader chain than a static mesh import: character rigging, scan-derived texture migration, a Unity real-time hair solution adapted from an existing demo workflow, hair motion, animation playback, and real-time rendering.

AccuRIG provided the fast body-rigging path for the cleaned prototype.

The existing character texture information was reorganized into the material structure required by the Unity prototype.

Used a Unity real-time hair solution based on an existing Unity demo workflow, with visible secondary motion during playback.

Used third-party video-based markerless motion capture derived from a filmed full-body performance to test animated playback.

What worked

The prototype proved that the captured person could become a rigged, textured, animated, hair-enabled real-time character. Performance and runtime feasibility were not the reasons the pipeline was abandoned.

Task 01D / Identity Failure

Recognizing that a technically functional character had failed the more important test

Once the Unity prototype was moving correctly, the main weakness became obvious: it no longer carried enough of Shininho’s specific presence. The mesh had become cleaner and easier to rig, but the simplification and reconstruction made it feel like a game character copied from Shininho rather than Shininho herself.

Rigging, rendering, hair behavior, and animation playback were all functional.

The result no longer preserved the subject’s specific age, facial character, and recognizable presence strongly enough.

Further polishing the reduced mesh could improve execution but could not restore identity information that had already been lost.

Stop promoting the prototype and return to the original high-resolution scan.

This became the central production lesson of the project: a real-time character can be technically successful and still fail if the pipeline destroys the identity that made the source valuable.
Task 01E / Wrap + MetaHuman Production Rebuild

Rebuilding from high-resolution source while gaining a production-ready facial and animation ecosystem

For the final character, I returned to the original high-resolution scan rather than the reduced Unity prototype. In Faceform Wrap, I aligned the scan to a MetaHuman-compatible template using correspondence points, resolved local mesh-fit problems, transferred the scan-derived surface information, and exported geometry suitable for the MetaHuman production workflow. The goal was to preserve the person while gaining a rig that could support Live Link, MetaHuman Animator, and Unreal performance assembly.

Original high-resolution scan, not the simplified feasibility mesh.

Source / template alignment with correspondence points, local fit correction, wrapped geometry output, and texture transfer.

Keeping the existing UV / texture information usable through the rebuild and solving hair integration around the new production character.

MetaHuman provided a much stronger downstream path for Live Link, MetaHuman Animator, and later performance capture integration.

Production result

The second pipeline recovered substantially more of Shininho’s recognizable identity while also making the character easier to drive, edit, and assemble with modern facial and body performance tools.

Chapter outcome

The first pipeline was valuable because it failed clearly: it proved that the real-time problem was solvable, then exposed identity preservation as the true production constraint. The final pipeline was redesigned around that discovery.

02 / LIKENESS

UV, Texture, Eye & Identity Preservation

Once the production topology changed, the problem was no longer simply “make the character detailed.” The goal was to preserve the visual evidence that made the character recognizably Shininho while solving the regions where the source data could not transfer cleanly.

Task 02A / UV & Texture Continuity

Keeping the original scan surface attached to a completely different production topology

Moving from the source scan into Wrap and MetaHuman changed the geometry structure, but the final character still needed the original skin information. One of the main production problems was therefore preserving the scan-derived UV / texture relationship through the topology rebuild rather than accepting a generic replacement material.

The face and body scan textures carried age, skin variation, and small visual cues that were important to likeness.

The production character used MetaHuman-compatible geometry rather than the source scan topology.

Used the Wrap workflow to move the source surface information onto the production character structure.

Checked the transferred surface under final real-time rendering rather than judging only the texture maps in isolation.

Task 02B / Scalp Recovery & Age Detail

Repairing source information that the scan itself never captured cleanly

Shininho wore a white head covering during the original scan, so the scalp / upper-head region did not contain the same usable photographic information as the exposed face. I repaired and extended that area in Photoshop with AI-assisted image expansion, then tuned the transferred surface so wrinkles and age detail remained readable in the final character.

White head covering obscured the scalp / hair-region texture information during capture.

Extended and reconstructed missing upper-head texture information in Photoshop using AI-assisted expansion and manual cleanup.

Adjusted contrast and sharpness where necessary so wrinkles did not disappear after transfer and real-time shading.

Preserve the subject’s age and identity rather than cosmetically smoothing the scan into a generic digital face.

Task 02C / Eye Alignment

Resolving the mismatch between a scan-derived custom head and the default MetaHuman eye setup

The source did not provide a directly reusable final eye solution, and the default MetaHuman eye placement did not sit naturally inside the custom scan-derived head. In some views the eyes could appear to protrude or feel spatially disconnected from the face.

The issue persisted as a spatial head/eye mismatch rather than simply a lighting artifact.

Iteratively adjusted eye-position values in Unreal until the eyes sat more naturally inside the head.

Tuned iris and general eye appearance so the eyes belonged more convincingly to the elderly scan-derived face.

This was an Unreal alignment and lookdev solution, not a claimed full custom eye-mesh reconstruction.

Task 02D / Hair Production Trade-off

Prioritizing identity and performance when groom integration remained unstable

Hair remained one of the more difficult parts of the production rebuild. The Unity prototype had demonstrated real-time hair motion, but the later MetaHuman production character introduced a different groom integration problem. Rather than allowing an imperfect groom to dominate the schedule and distract from the likeness work, the final presentation used a hat as a controlled art-direction solution.

Production judgment

The trade-off was deliberate: preserve the face, age, performance, and overall identity first; use a contained visual workaround for a lower-priority groom problem that had not reached the same level of stability.

Task 02E / Identity Validation

Judging the rebuild by whether it felt like the real person—not by polygon count

The quality benchmark changed after the Unity prototype. The question was no longer whether the character looked technically advanced; it was whether people who actually knew Shininho recognized her presence in the digital human. The rebuilt production version passed that test much more convincingly.

Technically functional, but felt like a generic game-character interpretation of the scan.

The high-resolution Wrap + MetaHuman rebuild retained substantially more of the subject’s recognizable facial character.

The artist—Shininho’s granddaughter—responded that the rebuilt character genuinely felt like Shininho.

The stronger identity was paired with easier performance integration through MetaHuman rigging, Live Link, and MetaHuman Animator.

Chapter outcome

The final likeness came from treating identity as data that had to survive every technical translation: source mesh separation, topology replacement, UV and texture transfer, eye alignment, real-time shading, and final performance.

Operating the OptiTrack session from performer preparation through solved skeleton data

I did not only receive motion-capture files downstream. I prepared the performers, attached the optical markers, directed the actor through the capture, configured and calibrated the OptiTrack system, performed the T-pose calibration, solved the actor skeleton in Motive, reviewed the result, and exported the captured performance as FBX for cleanup.

Placed markers directly on the actor’s mocap suit and checked the marker set before capture.

Handled OptiTrack setup and calibration, including performer T-pose calibration.

Built / solved the performer skeleton in Motive and reviewed tracking during the session.

Directed the performer through the required movement and decided when a take needed to be repeated.

Output

The primary body-performance data was an OptiTrack / Motive skeleton solve exported as FBX for MotionBuilder and Maya cleanup before final MetaHuman integration.

Task 03B / Retake Strategy

Using live tracking quality to decide when difficult floor movement needed another take

The hardest movement occurred when the performer moved down to or lay on the floor. Optical tracking could become unstable and the solved skeleton could visibly jump. When that failure was obvious during capture, I did not assume it could all be fixed later—I stopped and recorded a new take immediately.

Low-to-floor and lying movement created the most severe tracking instability.

Watched the real-time tracking / solve and identified visible jumps during capture.

Sent the performer back into the capture volume and recorded another take when the data was clearly unusable.

Even good retakes could retain localized errors that later required manual animation reconstruction.

The workflow separated problems that were cheaper to solve on stage from problems that were better repaired in animation. Severe tracking failure triggered a retake; localized residual errors were handled downstream.
Task 03C / MOVIN TRACIN Hand Supplement

Using MOVIN TRACIN where optical body capture was weakest: wrist and finger motion

OptiTrack remained the primary body source, but the optical setup was less convincing around the hands and fingers. Because the performer repeated the same choreography on the same timing, I captured / referenced MOVIN TRACIN data and used its wrist, hand, and finger motion as a complementary source where it produced a better result.

Task 03D / Facial Capture

Capturing facial performance independently with an iPhone mounted on a Rokoko helmet

Facial performance was captured as a separate asset. The iPhone was mounted to a Rokoko helmet / head rig and recorded through Live Link Face. I then used MetaHuman Animator’s depth/video-processing workflow to generate the production facial performance rather than treating the live ARKit-style preview as the final result.

Rokoko helmet / head-mounted support carrying the iPhone during facial acting.

Live Link Face on iPhone.

MetaHuman Animator depth/video processing for the production facial solve.

Face was captured separately, so the best facial take did not have to be the face recorded alongside the final body take.

Task 03E / Clap Sync & Take Mixing

Using a shared clap reference to combine the strongest body and facial performances

The clap was captured by both the body and facial recording setups. I used that common event as the timing anchor when assembling the performance, which made it possible to combine a strong OptiTrack body take with a different facial take when their performance quality differed.

Same clap event visible / recorded in both body and facial capture.

Selected the strongest cleaned body take rather than automatically keeping the take paired with the face.

Selected facial takes independently based on performance quality and likeness.

Aligned the independent streams in Unreal using the shared clap reference and final performance timing.

Editorial value

Body Take A + Face Take B could become one final character performance. Capture synchronization therefore supported performance editing, not merely technical alignment.

Chapter outcome

The capture workflow combined on-stage technical ownership with editorial flexibility: I operated and evaluated the optical capture directly, supplemented weak hand data with MOVIN TRACIN, captured the face independently, and preserved enough synchronization information to choose the best performance from each source.

Task 04A / MotionBuilder Review & Filtering

Using MotionBuilder to inspect the full mocap performance before detailed shot editing

MotionBuilder was the most efficient environment for reviewing the complete mocap data as a captured performance. I used it to identify global problems, apply motion cleanup, reduce unnecessary keys, resolve rotation issues, and plot / bake the cleaned result before moving into more detailed Maya animation work.

Evaluated the complete solved performance and located unstable sections before detailed manual editing.

Used Butterworth Filter, Smooth, and other filtering passes where the data contained high-frequency noise.

Used Key Reducer and Euler Filter where appropriate to reduce redundant keys and correct rotation discontinuities.

Used Plot / Bake workflows to commit the cleaned motion before the next stage.

Filtering was selective. A noisy curve could benefit from filtering; a fundamentally broken pose or tracking jump needed manual reconstruction instead.
Task 04B / Maya HumanIK & Animation Editing

Moving into Maya when animation needed direct artistic control

I preferred Maya for detailed animation work and performance restructuring. The body animation was brought through HumanIK, then edited with animation layers and curve / keyframe tools. Maya was also where I reorganized and combined animation sections into the required sequence rather than treating each mocap take as a fixed final shot.

Used Maya HumanIK as part of the body-animation retarget / preparation workflow.

Used animation layers and direct keyframe editing for localized corrections.

Reordered / combined animation sections in Maya to build the desired performance sequence.

Committed edited animation before export to Unreal for final MetaHuman retargeting.

Task 04C / Floor-Tracking Reconstruction

Reconstructing motion manually when even the retake still contained broken tracking

The most difficult cleanup case was the performer lying on the floor. Severe failures were retaken on stage, but some localized tracking damage still remained. When filtering could not recover the motion, I rebuilt the segment by hand rather than smoothing across the failure.

Copied the last reliable pose / motion before tracking broke.

Used the next reliable pose after the damaged section as the opposite boundary condition.

Inserted and adjusted intermediate keys by hand, then refined interpolation until the body read naturally through the missing segment.

Used the corresponding MOVIN TRACIN motion as an additional reference when useful.

Why this mattered

The repaired motion preserved the performer’s intent through a section where no single captured data stream was clean enough to use directly.

Task 04D / Error-Specific Cleanup

Choosing a different correction method for foot float, ankle twist, spikes, and tracking loss

The production data contained multiple failure types, and I avoided treating them all as the same “mocap noise” problem. Contact drift, rotation flips, isolated spikes, and missing tracking each required a different balance of filtering and manual animation.

Re-established contact while preserving the dancer’s weight transfer rather than locking the foot indiscriminately.

Used rotation diagnosis / Euler cleanup and manual correction on the affected range.

Removed isolated spikes while retaining the surrounding gesture and timing.

Used neighboring valid poses, manual keys, interpolation, retakes, and MOVIN TRACIN reference as appropriate.

Task 04E / Unreal Retarget & Rig Bake

Retargeting the cleaned HumanIK performance to MetaHuman and baking body and face independently

After Maya preparation, the body animation was brought into Unreal and retargeted to the production MetaHuman. In Sequencer, body and facial animation remained independent long enough to preserve editorial flexibility. Once the final take combination and timing were established, I baked the body and face separately onto their production rigs.

Maya HumanIK-prepared body animation → Unreal retarget → MetaHuman body rig.

MetaHuman Animator facial performance remained an independent facial track / rig.

Used the shared clap reference to align body and face and combine the chosen takes.

Baked body and facial animation separately to preserve control over each performance layer.

Task 04F / Final Rig Filtering

Applying a final smoothing pass after the performance was already assembled on the production rigs

The rig bake was not the end of cleanup. Once body and face were on their final rigs in Unreal, I used additional filtering to soften residual high-frequency movement while preserving the edited performance. This included Fourier- and Gaussian-based filtering in the final rig workflow.

Applied after body / face had been aligned and baked to the production rigs.

Used Fourier-based filtering where frequency-domain smoothing helped reduce residual jitter.

Used Gaussian smoothing where a softer local averaging response was appropriate.

Final filtering refined already edited motion; it did not replace manual reconstruction of broken tracking.

The cleanup stack therefore operated at several levels: better capture / retakes first, MotionBuilder filtering second, Maya manual reconstruction where necessary, then final rig-level filtering after Unreal assembly.
Task 04G / End-to-End Animation Path

Connecting capture, cleanup, retargeting, editorial selection, and final character animation

The complete body pipeline crossed several tools because each stage served a different purpose. Rather than forcing the entire job into one DCC, I used the environment that made each problem easiest to diagnose and control.

Final body path

OptiTrack / Motive → FBX → MotionBuilder cleanup → Maya HumanIK + animation editing → Unreal retarget → Sequencer → MetaHuman body rig bake → final filtering.

Final face path

Rokoko helmet + iPhone Live Link Face → MetaHuman Animator depth/video processing → Sequencer alignment → MetaHuman facial rig bake → final filtering.

Chapter outcome

The final animation was a deliberately reconstructed performance rather than a mocap export: I controlled capture quality on stage, selected and supplemented data across systems, filtered the solved motion, manually rebuilt broken poses, retargeted the result through HumanIK and Unreal, mixed independent body and facial takes, and finalized both performances on production MetaHuman rigs.

Task 05A / VR Comfort

Re-editing the performance for a viewer who controls their own gaze

The original animation was not designed around VR comfort. In Jooan, camera motion that felt acceptable on a flat screen could become uncomfortable in a headset, so I reduced viewer movement and let the character carry more of the spatial motion.

Compared faster and slower camera versions with approximately ten informal testers.

Reduced camera speed after the faster version produced a less comfortable viewing experience.

Kept the viewer position relatively stable while the character moved through the scene.

Evaluated the work in a PC-connected headset workflow rather than targeting standalone mobile VR.

Task 05B / Attention & Lighting

Using lighting as spatial direction in a 360° environment

A VR viewer can look away from the intended action at any time. I used darkness, contrast, and a dusk environment to make the character easier to locate without forcing the viewer through aggressive camera movement.

Skylight + HDRI-based dusk/sunset environment.

Used brightness and darkness to guide visual focus toward the character.

Avoided relying on cinematic framing that only works for a fixed 2D view.

Maintain legibility of the performance while preserving viewer agency.

Task 05C / Niagara

Building hand-driven ribbon trails as both motion accent and narrative effect

The hand trails were built as a new Niagara system rather than a baked effect. Separate emitters were attached to both hands, ribbon trails were timed to selected performance moments, and particle count was tuned with VR performance in mind.

Niagara effect built from scratch for the VR extension.

Separate emitters attached to the left and right hands.

Trail visibility was turned on/off at selected animation moments.

Particle count was adjusted to balance visual density with VR frame-rate constraints.

Narrative role

The ribbon trails amplified the dance into a more ritualistic, data-like visual language while still being driven directly by the performer’s hand motion.

Chapter outcome

Jooan demonstrates that the Shininho pipeline was reusable beyond a single rendered character: the same digital human and performance data could be re-staged for an immersive real-time context with new constraints around comfort, attention, and performance.

TAKEAWAY

The project became a full digital-human production pipeline: break down imperfect scan data, prototype in Unity, redesign around likeness when the prototype fails, operate and direct optical performance capture, supplement weak hand data with MOVIN TRACIN, reconstruct broken motion across MotionBuilder and Maya, retarget and bake independent body and facial performances in Unreal, and finally re-stage the character for VR.
