OVERVIEW

Building the character, facial system, rendering stack, and public interface from a portrait.

The project began without a living subject or 3D scan. The historical portrait showed a front-facing torso with the head turned away from full frontal view, so I treated the painted face as both likeness reference and spatial evidence. I reconstructed the head in ZBrush, transferred it through ZWrap into a production character, rebuilt UVs and skin maps, authored a custom ARKit-compatible blendshape rig, translated recorded iPhone coefficient data into Maya animation, built portrait-specific hair and clothing, adapted Unity's digital-human rendering structure, and coded the final museum kiosk interaction in C#.

01 / HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION

Reading a Historical Portrait as 3D Evidence

The portrait provided a recognizable face but not the multi-angle information of photography or scanning. I therefore reconstructed a plausible head by matching the specific painted view first, then testing whether that interpretation could survive rotation and animation.

HISTORICAL PORTRAIT → DIGITAL RECONSTRUCTION
MODEL · RIG · UV · FINAL SURFACE
Task 01A / Portrait Geometry Reading

Inferring depth from a frontal body and turned head

The reference portrait presents the body frontally while the head turns away from a fully frontal view. I used that specific pose as the primary reconstruction target and read facial depth, eye spacing, head rotation, silhouette, and painted light response as clues to the underlying 3D structure.

Historical portrait used as the main likeness reference.

Facial depth, nose projection, cheek volume, eye spacing, and visible silhouette.

Head rotation relative to the front-facing body provided a key spatial reference.

Painted light and shadow helped interpret where facial planes turned away from the viewer.

Task 01B / ZBrush Likeness Sculpt

Matching the portrait view before optimizing for production

I sculpted the likeness in ZBrush while repeatedly returning to the original portrait composition. The first goal was not generic anatomical realism, but making the reconstructed head read correctly from the historical reference angle before it was translated into a riggable mesh.

Match the historical portrait's recognizable proportions and head orientation.

Adjusted facial depth and proportions while comparing against the painted view.

Unseen depth and rear-head structure were reconstructed as plausible production geometry rather than claimed as historical fact.

A likeness sculpt strong enough to become the identity source for the production character.

Task 01C / ZWrap Transfer

Moving the reconstructed identity onto a controllable production structure

Once the likeness was established, I used ZWrap to transfer the sculpted facial identity into geometry that could continue through UV work, blendshape creation, rigging, and Unity rendering.

Result

The portrait reconstruction stopped being a static sculpture and became the identity source for an animation-ready custom digital human.

CHAPTER OUTCOME

The reconstruction stage separated two questions: what the historical portrait visually supports, and what the production character needs to exist convincingly beyond that single painted view.

Skin maps were developed with the final HDRP material structure in mind rather than as standalone texture paintings. Displacement / normal detail, roughness response, and skin color had to survive the transition from DCC authoring into the Unity digital-human shader stack.

Result

03 / CUSTOM FACIAL SYSTEM

52 ARKit Base Shapes → Expanded Custom Facial Rig → CSV Animation

The character predated the convenience of simply dropping a standard facial system onto a MetaHuman. I built the custom face around the 52 ARKit expression channels requested by the capture workflow, then expanded that base into a larger production shape library and translated recorded coefficient data into Maya animation.

PERFORMANCE CAPTURE → DIGITAL FACE
MOCAP STAGE / PERFORMANCE SOURCE
Task 03A / 52-Shape ARKit Foundation

Building the capture-compatible expression vocabulary first

I began by creating the 52 ARKit facial expression targets required by the mocap workflow. The target definitions followed the capture vendor's requested guide and ARKit/FACS reference material, giving the custom historical face a known coefficient vocabulary that could be driven by iPhone data.

52 ARKit expression channels established the initial facial-control vocabulary.

Shape definitions followed the capture vendor's guidance and ARKit / FACS mapping references.

Every expression had to be rebuilt specifically for the reconstructed historical head.

Create a direct bridge between mobile facial coefficients and the custom Maya character.

Task 03B / Expanded Shape Library

Extending the 52-channel foundation into a larger production rig

The ARKit set defined the minimum capture interface, but production facial deformation required additional targets. The final rig expanded the base expressions with mirrored, corrective, and combination shapes, resulting in an approximately 256-shape custom facial library.

52 ARKit-compatible base expressions.

Additional mirrored, corrective, and combined facial targets.

Approximately 256 custom facial blendshape targets in the production system.

The custom historical face needed deformation support beyond the raw capture-channel count.

Task 03C / ARKit CSV Import

Turning recorded iPhone coefficients into Maya animation

Facial performance was recorded on iPhone and exported as CSV coefficient data. I used a Python-based Maya import tool from an existing GitHub workflow: after selecting the face, the recorded data could be imported and applied to the corresponding blendshape channels.

Task 03D / Naming-Mismatch Debugging

Fixing a data-import failure caused by coefficient-name case mismatch

The import initially failed because the blendshape names in the recorded CSV did not match the case convention expected by the Maya rig / import tool. I normalized the affected coefficient names in the CSV to lowercase so the Python workflow could resolve the channels and apply the captured values correctly.

Recorded coefficient names did not map cleanly to the rig's blendshape names.

The mismatch came from uppercase / lowercase naming rather than from the facial values themselves.

Converted the relevant CSV blendshape names to the lowercase convention expected by the import workflow.

Recorded iPhone coefficient data could drive the custom Maya facial rig as intended.

Technical value

The problem was solved at the data-interface level rather than by manually recreating the performance after import failed.

Task 03E / Performance & Voice Split

Separating physical/facial acting from the final dubbed voice

One performer supplied the visible facial and bodily acting, while a separate professional voice actor provided the final dialogue afterward. The finished character therefore combined captured physical performance with an independently recorded final voice.

04 / HAIR & GARMENT

XGen Hair Cards, Rigged Beard & Offline Hanbok Simulation

Hair and costume were not solved through the same runtime strategy. The portrait-specific head treatment used authored card geometry and a relatively static Unity setup, while the Hanbok used offline Marvelous simulation because the real-time alternative did not provide convincing enough cloth behavior.

XGEN → HAIR CARDS / BEARD
HANBOK / COSTUME CHARACTER BREAKDOWN
Task 04A / XGen-to-Card Hair

Authoring portrait-specific hair in XGen, then converting it for real-time use

I first built the portrait-driven hair direction and shape in Maya / XGen, then converted that structure into card geometry for Unity. Because the character wore a hat and the historical silhouette was tightly controlled, the production hair did not require a full dynamic-groom system.

Maya / XGen used to establish hair direction and portrait-specific silhouette.

XGen result translated into real-time hair-card geometry.

Main hair remained attached / controlled rather than relying on a modern groom simulation stack.

Historical portrait and hat silhouette drove the final visual structure.

Task 04B / Beard Secondary Motion

Adding rigged secondary motion only where it materially improved the face

Facial hair needed more visible motion than the hat-covered hairstyle. I used a Unity-side rigged secondary-motion / cloth-style solution for the beard so it could react during performance without requiring the entire hair system to become dynamically simulated.

Task 04C / Marvelous Hanbok

Building traditional clothing around the animated production body

The Hanbok was built in Marvelous Designer using the production character as the garment avatar. The animated character motion was brought into the cloth workflow so the fabric could react to the actual performance rather than to a generic pose sequence.

Hanbok authored in Marvelous Designer.

Production Gang Se-hwang body used as the cloth-simulation character.

Shot performance imported to drive the garment simulation.

Historical clothing silhouette and believable cloth response over generic real-time garment motion.

Task 04D / Per-Shot Alembic Delivery

Choosing deterministic offline cloth when real-time simulation did not look convincing enough

Real-time cloth did not provide the visual realism required for the Hanbok, so I simulated the garment shot by shot in Marvelous Designer, exported the results as Alembic caches, imported them into Unity, and synchronized each cache to the corresponding performance through Timeline.

CHAPTER OUTCOME

Hair and clothing used different production representations because their visual requirements were different: card-based real-time character assets for the head, and deterministic simulated geometry for the Hanbok.

05 / UNITY HDRP LOOKDEV

Adapting Unity's Digital-Human Rendering Structure to a Custom Historical Character

The project used the structure of Unity's contemporary digital-human demo workflow as the rendering foundation. I followed that material architecture, then adapted its skin, eye, hair, and lighting behavior around a completely custom reconstructed head.

FINAL HDRP LOOKDEV
UNITY HDRP CHARACTER PREVIEW
Task 05A / Digital-Human Rendering Foundation

Reusing a proven material architecture while replacing the character-specific content

I followed the material and rendering structure demonstrated by Unity's Enemies-era digital-human project instead of reinventing the complete HDRP human shader stack. The technical foundation was reused; the reconstructed character, maps, proportions, and final visual response were custom to this project.

Unity digital-human / Enemies-style HDRP material architecture.

General material structure and rendering approach for high-quality real-time human skin and eyes.

Gang Se-hwang geometry, skin maps, displacement/detail, eyes, hair cards, beard, and final parameter tuning.

Apply a proven real-time human-rendering stack to a nonstandard historical character.

Task 05B / Skin SSS & Surface Response

Balancing subsurface response against painted historical skin detail

The skin used HDRP subsurface-scattering behavior together with roughness, specular, normal / detail-normal, and custom displacement information. The challenge was keeping the face human and dimensional without allowing the real-time SSS response to wash out the portrait-derived age and surface character.

HDRP SSS / diffusion-style skin response.

Roughness, specular, normal, and detail-normal tuning.

Custom displacement / facial-form information authored around the portrait reconstruction.

Maps and shader values judged under the final scene lighting, not only in neutral material preview.

Task 05C / Eye & Hair Integration

Making separate human-material systems read as one face

Eye/cornea response and hair-card shading were tuned against the same lighting environment as the skin. This prevented the eyes, beard, and hair from reading as disconnected material demonstrations attached to the reconstructed face.

Custom eye appearance tuned within the digital-human HDRP material structure.

Corneal highlight / reflective response balanced against the face lighting.

Card-based hair and beard shading tuned for translucency / specular response in the final scene.

Skin, eye, and hair lookdev evaluated together under the same HDRI / scene-lighting setup.

Task 05D / Final Lighting

Building the museum character as one real-time lighting system

HDRI, scene lights, material values, and final color response were tuned together until the custom historical head remained readable and human at the intended public viewing scale.

Result

A custom historical character rendered through a high-end real-time digital-human stack without depending on a stock MetaHuman character or prebuilt historical asset.

CHAPTER OUTCOME

The lookdev work demonstrates adaptation rather than reinvention for its own sake: I used an existing high-quality Unity rendering architecture, then rebuilt and tuned the character-specific layers required for this historical face.
